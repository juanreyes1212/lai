The nutrition goals panel had to answer one question: should the user
type their own daily targets, or should the app give them a starting
point? Both — but in that order, and the order matters.

## What didn't work

The first version was four number inputs: calories, protein, carbs, fat.
That's the "do it yourself" approach. It's honest, it's flexible, and
it's the wrong default. Nobody opens a fitness tracker for the first
time knowing they want 195 g of protein. They know they want to *get
leaner*, or *bulk*, or *maintain*.

Presenting four blank fields is asking the user to do macro math before
the app has done anything for them.

## Four presets, one tap

The second version puts four preset buttons at the top of the dialog:

- **Lean Muscle** — 2,800 kcal · 200 P · 300 C · 80 F
- **Clean Bulk** — 3,200 kcal · 220 P · 380 C · 95 F
- **Maintenance** — 2,400 kcal · 160 P · 270 C · 75 F
- **Cut** — 2,000 kcal · 180 P · 180 C · 65 F

Tapping any preset fills all four input fields. The inputs are still
editable — so the preset is a *starting point*, not a commitment. The
user can dial calories up 100 kcal and save. They didn't have to come
up with the other three numbers from scratch.

## Why the numbers are not personalized at this stage

I considered asking for height, weight, age, and activity level and
computing TDEE on the fly. I didn't, for three reasons:

1. **Body weight is already tracked elsewhere in the app.** Reusing it
   here would create a dependency between two screens for a one-time
   action. Not worth it.
2. **TDEE formulas are correct on average and wrong individually.**
   Mifflin-St Jeor will tell two people of the same stats to eat the
   same calories; one of them will gain, one will lose. The presets
   are *labels for intent*, not equations for metabolism.
3. **The AI recommendation layer adjusts the day-to-day numbers based
   on wearable data anyway.** The preset sets the baseline; the
   recommendation tunes it.

## What the macro rings do with the goal

Goals aren't decorative. Every macro ring on the food log fills against
its goal target. The calorie number shows `X of 2,800 kcal` with a
progress bar. When a ring crosses 100%, it turns red. When calories
cross the target, the calorie line goes red.

That color change is the entire signal. There's no notification, no
modal, no "you've exceeded your goal" toast. The ring changes color,
and the user sees it the next time they log food. Calm by default,
informative on glance.

## The "Goals" button placement

The button lives in the top-right corner of the Food Log card —
discoverable but not loud. Putting it in the global nav would have
implied "this is a thing you manage often." It's not. Most users will
tap a preset on day one and never open the panel again.

If they do open it — usually because their training phase changed —
the dialog is two clicks deep and one tap to a new preset. The whole
interaction is under 5 seconds.

## Persistence

Goals go to the database, not localStorage. The reason isn't
multi-device sync (this is a single-user MVP). It's that the AI
recommendation endpoint needs to read the baseline server-side. If the
baseline lived in the browser, the edge function would have to take it
as an argument on every request, which would mean the client could lie
to itself. Server-owned baseline = single source of truth for both the
UI and the AI layer.

## What this taught me about defaults

A blank form is honest but cruel. Four presets plus editable fields is
slightly less honest — you're nudging the user toward four particular
shapes of goal — but it's a much kinder default. The presets aren't
"the right answers." They're conversation starters.

For a tool people use daily, the day-one experience is the only one
that gets a fresh chance. Spend the design budget there.
